# **Physical Meets Digital: Physical Design with NFC Technology**

# **1 Wooden Card + 1 Acrylic Keychain**

Design and fabricate two personalized physical objects for someone other than yourself, including a hidden digital experience accessed through NFC technology.

# **The Project**

Create two designed objects for another person or people:

- One laser-cut wooden card with one programmed NFC tag attached.
- One laser-cut acrylic keychain with a lobster-claw clip attached and one programmed NFC tag attached.

You may design both objects for one recipient, or design each object for a different recipient.

You decide the shapes, cut geometry, graphics or finishes added after cutting, placement, and the digital experience.

## **Core Design Idea**

- **Design for another person.** Translate what you know about the recipient into intentional design decisions rather than designing what you would want for yourself.
- **Design for the fabrication process.** The laser is for cutting only: no laser engraving with raster graphics.
- **Treat NFC technology as a hidden layer of the experience.** The recipient should discover part of the experience when they tap the card, rather than seeing it on the surface. Think about the NFC tag as a way to trigger a hidden, digital experience. It is a portal to the digital world. How can the physical object you create be a prompt for the digital experience?

## **Production Constraint: 5 Minutes Maximum Laser Time**

Your total laser-cutting job must take no longer than five minutes. Because you do not yet have a reliable way to predict exact cutting time, part of the design process is experimentation: observe what takes time, revise your creative goals, and learn what can be accomplished within the limit. The five-minute limit is part of the design problem.

## **Who Are You Designing For?**

You must design for someone other than yourself. You may choose one recipient for both objects or two recipients, one for each object. For each object, be prepared to explain:

- Who the recipient is and what you know about them.
- What they might want to keep, carry, wear, display, or use.
- What visual language, form, and materials make sense for them.
- What they will see immediately and what they will discover only by tapping.

# **Learning Objectives**

By completing this project, you should be able to:

- Translate knowledge about another person into physical and digital design decisions.
- Design vector cut geometry for efficient laser fabrication within a fixed production constraint.
- Learn how to use the drill press.
- Use the drill press safely to create a controlled chamfer in acrylic.
- Program, test, and integrate an NFC tag that opens an accessible URL.
- Evaluate an object from the recipient’s perspective, including size, use, durability, accessibility, and meaning.

# **Project Process**

## **1. Understand NFC**

NFC stands for Near Field Communication. An NFC tag contains a small chip and antenna and does not require a battery. A compatible phone can read the tag when you bring it very close. In this project, the tag will contain a URL that opens the digital experience you design.

## **2. Consider Mystery, Discovery, and the Hidden Digital Layer NFC Provides**

Unlike a printed URL or QR code, NFC does not immediately reveal where it leads. That creates design space. Ask: What does the recipient understand just by looking at the object, and what do they discover only when they tap? The hidden layer might communicate something personal, surprising, useful, or caring that would not make sense to put directly on the surface.

For each recipient, write one or two sentences explaining the relationship between what is visible and what is hidden.

## **3. Decide Where the NFC Tag Will Lead**

For this assignment, encode a URL on the NFC tag. The URL may lead to an online experience chosen for the recipient, such as a song, playlist, photo album, video, map location, message, or another web-accessible destination. The tag stores the link, not the media itself.

Consider access. A URL that works for you may not work for the recipient. Think about what happens if they do not have a particular app or account, are not logged in, or open the link in a browser. Before programming the tag, test the URL in a private/incognito browser where you are not logged in. If possible, have someone else test it on another device. Ask: If I handed this to the recipient right now, could they tap it and get the intended experience without my help?

## **4. Consider the Recipient Experience**

Imagine the object in the recipient’s hands. Consider what they see, hold, tap, and discover. Decide whether the tap location should be obvious or whether some mystery is appropriate. Consider comfort, durability, and whether the object communicates enough without over-explaining.

## **5. Decide Where the Object Will Live**

Where will the object actually be carried, worn, stored, or displayed: pocket, keys, wrist, bag, wallet, desk, nightstand, or somewhere else? That context should affect dimensions, shape, thickness, edges, attachment points, and durability. Measure or make a physical mock-up rather than judging size only on screen.

## **6. Inspect and Measure the Materials**

Experiment but **DO NOT GUESS**. Measure the actual NFC tag, wood, acrylic, and hardware. Make sure the tag fits the wooden card. For the acrylic keychain, consider the available material size, attachment-hole diameter, clearance around the hole, and how the lobster claw will fit and move. Physical parts impose constraints; good design responds to them.

## **7. Develop Several Concepts**

Do not cut the first idea you have. Sketch multiple options. Explore shape, cut geometry, attachment-hole placement, NFC placement, visible versus hidden information, and how the object will be used. Treat the attachment hole as part of the composition.

## **8. Add Other Creative Practices**

The laser creates the platform, but it does not have to be the final step. You are welcome to paint, stain, collage, draw, illustrate, wood-burn, add metalwork, apply graphic design, or otherwise modify and finish the card or keychain as an intentional choice for the recipient. The five-minute laser limit does not limit the complexity of the finished object.

## **9. Prepare the Digital Cut Files**

Work at actual size. Your laser files should contain cut paths only: no raster graphics. Keep geometry appropriate to the material and the five-minute cutting limit.

## **10. Complete a Pre-Fabrication Check**

Before cutting, review:

- Overall dimensions and intended use.
- Fit of the NFC tag and hardware.
- Cut geometry and any fragile or narrow areas.
- Strength and clearance around the keychain attachment hole.
- Orientation and how the object will hang, sit, or be held.
- Whether the design can reasonably be fabricated within the five-minute cutting limit.

## **11. Peer Feedback and Testing**

Share your design with classmates before final fabrication and NFC installation. Ask for feedback on size, usability, durability, and the relationship between the visible object and hidden digital experience. Have classmates test your URL on their devices, and help test theirs in return. Giving constructive feedback and helping classmates test and troubleshoot are part of the assignment.

## **12. Laser Cut the Wood and Acrylic**

Laser-cut the wooden card and inspect its shape and edges. Test the NFC tag placement before installation. If something is wrong, revise before recutting. Then cut the acrylic keychain with its attachment hole. Inspect for cracks or weak areas and test how it will hang. Do not attach the hardware yet; chamfer the hole first.

## **13. Chamfer the Acrylic Attachment Hole**

Using the drill press and chamfering bit as demonstrated in class, support the acrylic, keep your hands clear, and remove only a small, controlled bevel around the attachment hole. Removing too much material can weaken or crack the acrylic. Follow all studio safety practices and class demonstrations.

## **14. Program the NFC Tag**

Use NFC Tools by Wakdev. Download only the official app from the Apple App Store or Google Play Store; do not download APKs or copies from third-party download sites. The free version is sufficient for this project.

1. Open NFC Tools and choose Write.
2. Choose Add a record.
3. Choose URL/URI and paste your tested web link.
4. Confirm the record, then choose Write.
5. Hold the phone near the NFC tag until the app confirms that writing succeeded.
6. Move the tag away and close the app.
7. Test the tag normally by bringing a compatible phone near it and confirming that the intended URL opens.

Do not lock the tag or make it read-only. Leave it rewritable. Test the destination while logged out or in a private/incognito browser, and if possible, test it on another device.

## **15. Install, Assemble, and Test Again**

Install the programmed NFC tag into the wooden card, then test it again. Attach the lobster-claw hardware to the chamfered acrylic keychain and check security, movement, and how it hangs.

## **16. Final Evaluation**

Evaluate both objects from the recipient’s perspective. Ask whether each object functions, holds up, feels intentional for that person, and makes sense in the place where it will live. Confirm that the hidden URL experience works without your help and that the relationship between the visible object and hidden content feels purposeful.

# **Class Presentation**

Present your finished work to the class in a format of your choice. Your presentation should help the class understand why these objects and the hidden digital experience make sense for the recipient or recipients. Include:

- Who each object is for and what you considered about that person.
- Where the object is intended to live or how it is intended to be used.
- Why you chose the form, dimensions, visual treatment, and finishes.
- What is visible and what is hidden, and how those two layers connect.
- Why NFC creates intrigue, discovery, care, or meaning beyond simply printing the URL.
- Other creative practices you applied, such as painting, collage, wood burning, metalwork, illustration, staining, graphic design, or another relevant practice, and how those choices support the design.

# **Idea Starters**

These are examples, not templates. Notice that each idea begins with a particular person, relationship, habit, memory, or audience. Start by asking who you are designing for and what you know about them; let that knowledge shape the object and the hidden experience.

## **Keychain Ideas**

**1. Play This When...**

Your younger sister is about to leave for college. You remember your own first weeks away: figuring out the dining hall, meeting roommates, getting lost on campus, and suddenly missing home, a place you couldn't wait to leave. You and your sister have always traded songs, in a bratty way and sometimes in a nice way, especially on long drives together waiting to get over the Cajon Pass. She'll be at school in Santa Cruz so you won't see each other like you used to. You make a keychain for her new dorm keys. Tapping it opens a playlist called “Play This When You Miss Home,” filled with songs that connect to your shared memories.

**2. The Special Place Keychain**

You and your best friend have returned to the same beach, park, overlook, or neighborhood spot for years. Maybe it is where you went after finals, where your group always met in the summer, or where you had an important, and difficult, conversation. Design the keychain around details of that particular place rather than a generic symbol. Tapping it could open a shared photo album, a map, or a collection of memories from the times you spent there.

**3. The Good-Luck Charm**

Your cousin is auditioning for school plays and always calls you when their nerves kick in. You know the backstage routines, phrases, or jokes that calm them down because you have talked them through auditions before. Make a small object they can keep on a backpack or keys. Tapping it could open a short video of you telling the joke that makes them crack up so they can watch it right before an audition.

**4. The Inside-Joke Keychain**

You and a friend still laugh about a ridiculous moment that nobody else would understand: a phrase someone misheard, a disastrous road trip stop, or a photo that has become part of your shared vocabulary. Design the physical keychain as a subtle reference, not an explanation of the joke. Tapping it reveals the photo, video, audio clip, or other artifact that makes the reference meaningful to that particular person.

**5. The Everyday Keychain**

Your dad is always working on old cars, and some of your memories with him involve standing in the garage, handing him tools, or holding a flashlight while he worked under the hood. Now that you are away at college, you do that together less often. Design something durable and appropriately sized for the large set of keys he carries, perhaps drawing from a car or detail he loves. The hidden link could open photos of projects you have worked on together or another collection connected to that shared interest.

## **Card Ideas**

**1. A Music Card**

Your grandmother introduced you to a musician you still listen to. Maybe every visit to her house included records playing in the kitchen, or a particular song always came on during drives together down the 15 freeway. Design the card using visual clues that recall that shared ritual without simply naming the musician. Tapping reveals the song or playlist and perhaps a short message explaining what that memory means to you.

**2. A Memory Card**

A close friend is moving away. They are the kind of person who saves ticket stubs, photos, notes, and other physical reminders. Create a small collage, drawing, or other physical composition that includes the wooden card with an NFC tag attached. Tapping it opens a much larger photo album, video collection, or set of memories of a road trip you took with them.

**3. A Secret-Message Card**

Your younger brother has had a difficult semester, but you know he would hate having an obviously sentimental message sitting out where everyone could see it. Design the visible card around something he genuinely likes so it can live naturally on his desk or shelf. Only when he taps it does he discover the personal message from you. NFC matters here because it lets the emotional layer remain private to your "super tough" brother until he chooses to open it.

**4. An Experience Card**

Your mom has repeatedly told you she does not need more stuff in her house. Some of your favorite times together have been hiking, seeing movies, exploring neighborhoods, and trying new food. Design a physical card around one of those shared interests with her. Tapping it reveals an outing or experience you planned for the two of you. The object is not just another possession for her to keep track of. It becomes a keepsake from time you will share together.

**5. A LEGO Build**

Your niece loves LEGO, but what excites her most is inventing her own builds rather than simply following a boxed set. Give her the wooden card with a small bag or box of carefully chosen LEGO pieces. Paint, collage, or shape the card to give a clue without revealing the solution. Tapping could open a custom build challenge, instructions you created, or a video introducing the challenge. There may not even be one correct build: the larger gift is an experience designed around the fact that she loves making things.

## **Experience Idea**

**The Hidden Trail: A Scavenger Hunt or Interactive Adventure**

Maybe your friends love puzzles, your younger cousins love scavenger hunts, or you want to create something playful for people in the class. The first card or keychain becomes the starting point. Tapping opens a URL with a clue, creative prompt, riddle, image, or instruction that sends the player somewhere else. Another NFC tag can reveal the next step, creating a scavenger hunt, puzzle trail, geocache-style experience, interactive story, or chain of creative challenges. If your concept genuinely benefits from multiple NFC tags, ask me; I can provide additional tags. Use extra technology only when it makes the experience more interesting or meaningful.

All of your creative practices and finishing choices are intentional, well executed, and support the design for the recipient.

You give useful feedback, help test classmates’ URLs/designs, and use peer testing to improve your own work.

**8. Presentation and Design Rationale**

The presentation clearly explains who the work is for, key physical and creative choices, the visible/hidden relationship, and why NFC is meaningful for the recipient.
